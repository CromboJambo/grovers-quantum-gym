Dustin, that last sentence is the interesting one.

“Maybe we're just celebrating the successful collapse of a state we couldn't actually witness.”

I think you're describing something that sits at the intersection of learning theory, scientific reasoning, and information theory. And there's a real idea in there that we can sharpen without losing the philosophical part.

## 1\. Being wrong generates information. Being right often terminates the search.

Imagine you're trying to figure out why a machine isn't working.

You have ten possible explanations. You test one, and it fails.

You've learned something. That explanation becomes less likely, and the remaining possibilities become more interesting.

Then you find a solution. The machine works.

You stop investigating.

But notice the asymmetry:

- A failed hypothesis tells you that your model was wrong in a particular way.
- A successful intervention tells you that something worked under the conditions you tested.
- Neither necessarily tells you that you understand the entire system.

Success can close the investigation prematurely. Failure tends to reopen it.

This connects to the scientific idea of falsifiability: a theory becomes meaningfully testable when we can specify observations that would count against it.

But there's a caveat. Failure isn't automatically informative. A badly designed experiment can fail without telling you much. And a successful experiment can teach you a tremendous amount when it distinguishes between competing explanations.

The key isn't that wrong is always better than right.

It's that an outcome becomes especially informative when it eliminates possibilities we previously considered plausible.

## 2\. Your idea of a successful collapse

Let's look at your phrase through the lens of uncertainty.

Before an experiment, you have a set of possible explanations. You don't know which one is correct.

You perform an experiment. The result eliminates some possibilities, and your uncertainty decreases.

In information theory, the information gained from an observation can be expressed as the reduction in uncertainty between what you believed before and what you believe afterward.

In a simplified model:

$$
\text{Information gained}
=
H(\text{before})-H(\text{after})
$$

Here, $H$ represents entropy: a measure of uncertainty.

Your phrase successful collapse suggests that the system of possibilities has narrowed to a state where you can act confidently.

But there's a subtle distinction.

You might have reduced uncertainty because you genuinely discovered how the world works. Or you might have reduced uncertainty because you stopped considering alternatives.

Those can feel identical from the inside.

### Two kinds of closure

Epistemic closure

You've gathered enough evidence to justify a conclusion, within a defined scope.

Psychological closure

You feel that the question has been answered, so you stop searching.

The danger is mistaking the second for the first.

Science tries to manage this problem through replication, criticism, uncertainty estimates, and the possibility of revisiting conclusions. Those practices don't eliminate premature closure, but they help prevent one person's confidence from becoming the final authority.

## 3\. Maybe we're not lazy. Maybe we've reached a stable state.

Here's where your alternative explanation gets interesting.

Learning takes effort. Maintaining uncertainty takes effort, too. When we find an explanation that predicts what happens next, we gain the ability to act without reconsidering everything from scratch.

That's useful. We couldn't function if we reopened every question every morning.

So the brain needs some mechanism for saying, "This is good enough. Proceed."

You could think of that as a state transition:

Plain text

`Uncertainty     ↓ Exploration     ↓ Evidence     ↓ A workable model     ↓ Reduced uncertainty     ↓ Action without repeated investigation`

The final transition is not necessarily laziness. It's the payoff from learning. We stop spending resources on a question because the current model is sufficiently useful.

The problem appears when the model becomes stable for reasons unrelated to its accuracy.

For example, a process can become accepted because it works, because everyone has learned it, because changing it is expensive, or because the people who benefit from it have no incentive to question it.

Those are very different reasons to stop looking.

And this brings us back to your earlier argument about who defines the game. A system's definition of "solved" determines which questions remain open and which become invisible.

## 4\. There's a deeper issue: you can't observe the entire state of a complex system.

In control theory, an important distinction exists between a system's actual internal state and the observations available to an observer.

You might observe that a machine is producing acceptable output without knowing every internal condition that produced it.

You might observe that a company is profitable without knowing every long-term cost it is accumulating.

You might observe that a scientific prediction works without knowing whether your explanation of the underlying mechanism is correct.

In each case, you have evidence about the system, but not complete access to its state.

This is the problem of observability: can the internal state of a system be inferred from the outputs we can measure?

Your idea of celebrating an unobservable collapse captures something important here. We see the uncertainty resolve enough for us to proceed, but we don't necessarily see every hidden variable that made the resolution possible.

There's one correction I'd make, though: the state doesn't have to collapse in reality. Sometimes only our uncertainty collapses. The world may remain complicated while our model becomes simpler.

That difference is crucial.

## 5\. What if we treated every solution as a temporary truce?

Imagine an engineering culture where solving a problem doesn't mean declaring the subject finished. It means recording three things:

- What we now believe: the current best explanation.
- What the evidence actually establishes: the scope within which that explanation has been tested.
- What could make us reconsider: observations that would invalidate or weaken the conclusion.

The solution becomes actionable without becoming unquestionable.

That's a powerful principle for software, accounting, science, and personal reasoning. It allows you to stop investigating without pretending that you've eliminated every possible source of error.

It also avoids the opposite failure: endless skepticism that prevents you from ever doing anything.

You don't need absolute certainty to act. You need a defensible level of confidence, an understanding of the consequences if you're wrong, and a way to detect when your assumptions stop holding.

## The idea I would keep from your theory

Learning isn't just the accumulation of correct answers. It's the progressive improvement of our ability to distinguish between what we know, what we merely believe, and what we haven't yet learned how to observe.

And maybe your successful collapse is the moment when uncertainty has decreased enough that action becomes possible.

The question is whether we preserve any trace of the uncertainty that existed before that moment.

Because if we don't, future generations inherit the conclusion without inheriting the path that justified it. They get the answer, but lose the ability to tell when the answer no longer applies.

That seems especially relevant to the systems you've been thinking about: keeping the history of decisions, preserving the chain of evidence, and making it possible to reconstruct why a result was accepted rather than merely trusting the current state.

One last distinction, though, because I think it might push your idea further:

What if the most valuable thing we preserve isn't the answer or even the history of how we found it, but the set of alternatives we ruled out—and the evidence that ruled them out?

That would turn a solution from a terminal state into a map of the uncertainty that existed before it.
